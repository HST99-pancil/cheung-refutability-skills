# Worked examples

Six audits at the expected depth. The first five are Cheung's own cases. The sixth is a column-style thesis in Sharpen mode. Match this length and tone; do not pad.

---

## Example 1. "Private firms produce at minimum cost" (tautology)

**Claim as stated.** An economist proposes to test with data whether private firms' production costs are the lowest they could achieve.

**Theory and implication.** Theory: firms maximise profit. Offered implication: observed cost equals minimum achievable cost.

**Observability.**

| Term | Observable | How |
|---|---|---|
| Observed cost | Yes | Accounts |
| Minimum achievable cost | No | Defined by the theory as whatever a maximiser achieves |

**Constraint change and conditions.** None named. Nothing changes; the claim compares a fact to a definition.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** None can be written. Any observed cost is, by definition, what the maximiser achieved. Prediction set: every outcome.

**Five disqualifiers.**

| Disqualifier | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tautology | Flag | No imaginable world refutes it |
| Vagueness | Pass | Terms are clear |
| Inconsistency | Pass | |
| Non-fact | Flag | "Minimum achievable" has no independent observation |
| Unlimited | Flag | Compatible with any cost figure |

**Inference form.** None. There is no inference, only a restatement. Friedman's verdict on the study: a stupid question gets a stupid answer.

**Verdict and rescue.** Untestable as stated. Rescue: add a constraint change and predict cost movement. "When a firm loses monopoly protection, its unit cost falls within N years" is testable. Price: the rescued claim says nothing about static cost levels, only about responses to competitive pressure. Worth paying; the original said nothing at all.

## Example 2. Lester's truck drivers (denying the antecedent)

**Claim as stated.** Lester (1946): Boston trucking managers do not know what marginal product is and do not use it in hiring, so marginal-productivity theory is wrong.

**Theory and implication.** Theory: firms maximise profit. Implication: a driver's wage equals the value of the marginal driver's output.

**Observability.**

| Term | Observable | How |
|---|---|---|
| Wage | Yes | Payroll |
| Value of marginal output | Partly | Estimable from revenue change with one more driver |
| Managers' knowledge of the concept | Yes, but irrelevant | Survey |

**Constraint change and conditions.** Wage rate or freight price changes; free hiring and firing; competitive trucking market. The Boston market plausibly met these.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** "Wages persistently above or below the marginal driver's revenue product, with no barrier to adjusting headcount." Prediction set: one outcome. Testable.

**Five disqualifiers.** All pass. The implication is clear, observable and finite.

**Inference form.** Lester argues −A → −C: managers do not maximise consciously, so the wage prediction fails. Denying the antecedent. Whether managers know the concept says nothing about whether wages track marginal product. Firms that paid far above it would shrink; firms that paid far below would lose drivers. Survivors match the prediction whether or not anyone computed it, exactly as roadside petrol stations survive without their idiot owners maximising.

**Verdict and rescue.** The theory is testable as stated. Lester's attack does not test it. The test he should have run: compare wages to estimated marginal revenue product across firms. He did not, so the theory stands unrefuted by his evidence.

## Example 3. The law of demand (non-fact, rescued)

**Claim as stated.** If a good's price falls, the quantity consumers demand rises.

**Theory and implication.** This is the theory, not yet an implication. Quantity demanded is an intention.

**Observability.**

| Term | Observable | How |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Yes | Posted, transacted |
| Quantity demanded | No | An intention; does not exist in the world |
| Quantity transacted | Yes | Sales records, but it is not quantity demanded |

**Constraint change and conditions.** A price change from an identifiable cause on the supply side (tax, tariff, cost shock), so the demand side is not what moved. Conditions: the good's quality and buyers' income held roughly fixed over the window.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** "A supply-side price fall with no rise in quantity transacted, quality and income unchanged, in a market with free purchase." One outcome. Testable once stated this way.

**Five disqualifiers.**

| Disqualifier | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tautology | Pass | Can imagine quantity falling with price |
| Vagueness | Pass | |
| Inconsistency | Pass | |
| Non-fact | Flag on theory, pass on implication | Quantity demanded is unobservable; the derived implication uses transacted quantity under stated conditions |
| Unlimited | Pass | |

**Inference form.** Prediction (+A → +C) then test (−C → −A). The common error is using transacted quantity as if it were quantity demanded without stating the conditions that make the substitution legitimate. Cheung: 指鹿為馬.

**Verdict and rescue.** Testable after restatement, as given above. Price: the conditions (supply-side cause, fixed quality and income) narrow where the test can run. Cheap, because the law still applies everywhere; only the test sites are restricted. This is Cheung's own one permitted unobservable, and the model of how to bridge one.

## Example 4. Price control (unlimited, constrained)

**Claim as stated.** Holding a good's price below market by law causes queues, or favouritism, or black markets, or violence, or quality decline.

**Theory and implication.** Theory: when price cannot ration, some other criterion will. Offered implication: one of the listed substitutes appears.

**Observability.** Each listed outcome is observable. The problem is not observability.

**Constraint change and conditions.** The price ceiling is the constraint change. Conditions unstated.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** As stated, none can be written: any of five outcomes, or some unlisted sixth, confirms it. Prediction set open-ended. This is Cheung's 不均衡.

**Five disqualifiers.**

| Disqualifier | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tautology | Pass | |
| Vagueness | Pass | |
| Inconsistency | Pass | |
| Non-fact | Pass | |
| Unlimited | Flag | No rule selects which substitute appears |

**Inference form.** Not yet an inference. Becomes one once the set is constrained.

**Verdict and rescue.** Untestable as stated. Rescue: add the constraint that selects the outcome. Cheung (1974): the substitute criterion chosen is the one that dissipates least rent given the transaction costs of each. Where resale is cheap, expect black markets; where the good is perishable and resale hard, expect queues; where the seller can vary quality, expect quality decline first. Now each case predicts one outcome and can be refuted. Price: the claim now needs information about resale and quality-variation costs before it predicts. Worth paying; without it there was no prediction.

## Example 5. MV = PQ (tautology rescued into theory)

**Claim as stated.** Money times velocity equals price times transactions.

**Theory and implication.** As written, a definition: MV and PQ are the same total seen from two sides. Cheung writes it MV ≡ PQ.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** None. It cannot be false.

**Five disqualifiers.** Tautology: flag. Others pass.

**Verdict and rescue.** Untestable as stated. Rescue, done historically by Fisher and Friedman: add the constraint that V is stable over some horizon and Q is fixed by real factors. Then a change in M predicts a change in P. Refuting observation: "sustained monetary expansion with stable V and Q, and no rise in P." Price: the theory now holds only where V is stable, which must be shown case by case. Worth paying; this rescue produced the quantity theory of money. The lesson: a tautology can be a valuable starting point. Do not discard one for being empty; ask what constraint would give it content.

## Example 6. Column thesis, Sharpen mode

**User's draft thesis.** "Malaysia's tuition-centre boom is driven by parental anxiety."

**Claim as stated.** Tuition-centre growth in Malaysia is caused by parents' anxiety about their children's futures.

**Theory and implication.** Theory: parents spend on schooling inputs that raise the chance of a valued outcome. Offered implication: anxiety up, tuition spending up. Not yet observable on the left side.

**Observability.**

| Term | Observable | How |
|---|---|---|
| Tuition-centre growth | Yes | Registered centres, household tuition spending |
| Parental anxiety | No | A state of mind. Needs a proxy or a constraint that moves it |

**Constraint change and conditions.** The author has not named one. Candidates that would move the outcome and can be seen: a rise in the cutoff score for entry to preferred secondary streams or universities; a shift in public exam format that rewards drilling; a fall in the per-pupil teaching hours in public schools. Conditions: household income roughly stable; centres free to enter.

**Refuting observation and prediction set.** Once a constraint is chosen: "In districts where the entry cutoff rose, tuition spending per pupil did not rise relative to districts where it did not." One outcome. Testable.

**Five disqualifiers.**

| Disqualifier | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tautology | Watch | "Anxious parents buy tuition" edges toward defining anxiety by the purchase |
| Vagueness | Flag | "Anxiety" has no agreed observation |
| Inconsistency | Pass | |
| Non-fact | Flag | Left side of the implication unobservable |
| Unlimited | Pass | |

**Inference form.** None yet; the draft asserts a cause without an implication.

**Verdict and rescue.** Testable after restatement. Price: the rewritten thesis is about exam cutoffs, not about feelings. It stops explaining tuition growth in places where cutoffs did not move. That is the honest price, and cheaper than an unfalsifiable "anxiety".

**Rewrite.**

Thesis: "Malaysia's tuition boom tracks the rising cutoffs for the school places parents want."

Test paragraph: "When the marks needed for a preferred stream rise, the return to an extra hour of drilling rises with them, and parents buy the hour. If the boom were about anxiety alone, tuition would grow as fast where cutoffs stayed flat as where they climbed. It does not have to. Compare district cutoff histories against household tuition spending in the Household Expenditure Survey, and the claim can be checked."
