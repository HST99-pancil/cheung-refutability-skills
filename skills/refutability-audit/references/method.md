# The method: Cheung's 科學的方法, condensed

Source: 張五常《經濟解釋》卷一《科學說需求》第一章〈科學的方法〉, 4th edition (2017). Section numbers below refer to that chapter. The four-form logic grid is standard hypothetical syllogism, as taught in the "3-step logic" lecture note the skill's author used.

## The three commitments of any empirical science (§1)

1. Phenomena exist by subjective judgment, but people must agree they exist. What cannot win agreement (supernatural powers, God) is outside science, not meaningless.
2. Every agreed phenomenon has a regularity, however hard to find.
3. Nothing happens without a cause. Inference (推測) and explanation are the same act with different timing.

## Facts cannot explain facts (§2)

Regularities among facts tell us "that", never "why". Rain with clouds, wheat in soil, prosperity with property rights: none explains the other. Two reasons theory is needed:

- Countless regularities. If each explained itself, every science would be a heap of unrelated "theories" with no general power.
- Regularities shift with conditions. Feathers fall, but rise in wind. Stones do not. Deciding how to classify requires a principle, and that principle is a theory.

Marshall, quoted by Cheung: the most reckless theorists are those who let facts speak for themselves while silently choosing which facts, then argue "after this, therefore because of this".

## The two extremes: ad hoc theory and tautology (§3)

**Ad hoc theory** (特殊理論). A refuted theory saved by adding conditions until it explains exactly one case. Cheung's parable: explain lower weight on a mountain by cold; refuted in a cold room; add wind; refuted; add slope; refuted; add altitude; the mountain is rebuilt in the lab and the theory is "confirmed". Not too little content but too much, so any change refutes it.

**Tautology** (套套邏輯). A statement that cannot even be imagined wrong. "Four-legged animals have four legs." Universally true, empty. Tautologies are often hard to see:

- "Smoking maximises the smoker's interest." Once maximisation covers all behaviour it explains none.
- An economist testing whether firms minimise cost tested a definition. Friedman: a stupid question gets a stupid answer.
- A prize-winning Harvard dissertation Alchian's review showed to be tautological throughout.

**Rescue principles.**
- Any refuted theory can be saved by adding conditions. It often should be. But rescue has a price, measured in lost generality. Do not pay too much.
- A tautology can become a theory by adding constraints. MV = PQ is a definition; Fisher and Friedman made it the quantity theory by specifying when V is stable. The Coase Theorem is called a tautology by critics; its value lies in how skilfully constraints are added to it.
- Kessel: with no theory in hand you cannot win any argument. Explaining one phenomenon beats explaining none.
- Progress is broader theories replacing narrower ones, not correct replacing wrong. No theory is immune to replacement.

## Refutability is the criterion (§4)

Asked what matters most in scientific method, Cheung answers: a theory's inferences must be capable of refutation by facts. Science does not seek to be right or wrong. It seeks refutability. An inference that could be refuted but has not been counts as confirmed, and the phenomenon counts as explained. A theory is judged by explanatory usefulness, never by "right or wrong".

Two choices when refuted: abandon, or add conditions at a price.

**The logic of testing.** If A implies B, then not-B implies not-A. Rain implies clouds; no clouds implies no rain; rain without clouds refutes. Testing means seeking not-B with A present.

**The fallacy of denying the antecedent** (否決前事的謬誤). Arguing not-A therefore not-B. "People do not always maximise, therefore they need not work hard." Lester (1946) surveyed Boston trucking firms, found managers had never heard of marginal product, and declared marginal-productivity theory wrong. Twenty years of debate followed.

**False assumptions can yield true implications.**
- Idiots build petrol stations at random: on mountains, in forests, at sea, by roads. Only roadside stations survive. The maximising assumption is false of them; the prediction about surviving stations is correct and tested.
- Chinese astronomers predicted eclipses well. Children heard the 天狗吃太陽 legend. If a prediction from an absurd assumption is accurate and unrefuted, accept it until a broader theory replaces it. "There is no heavenly dog, so eclipses cannot be predicted" is denying the antecedent.

## Vagueness and inconsistency (§5)

Beyond tautology, four more forms are unrefutable. Two here.

**Vagueness.** Cheung's "Coase's Second Law", from Coase (1960) on Pigou: a vague idea can never be clearly shown wrong. To be refutable a theory must clearly show how it could be wrong. Examples: Marx's surplus value (read as rent, interest, profit or nothing); Knight's risk versus uncertainty; parts of Keynes's General Theory; Bentham's utility until Alchian asked "what is utility?". Nobody ever tested Marx because vague theories cannot be tested, and unfalsifiable theories are then mistaken by followers for absolute truth.

**Inconsistency.** A self-contradictory statement is neither empty nor vague; it is meaningless, and logic can derive anything from it. "An all-white wall has black dots." Economics examples: Issawi assumed maximising landlords then wrote that he did not assume they respond to income opportunities; Marshall knew fixed rent yielded more than sharecropping yet did not let landlords choose it; Baumol's sales-maximiser may not trade a little sales for much profit; Hicks's inferior-goods result came from a two-good model that cannot produce it. Direct contradictions are easy to catch. Indirect ones, after several inference steps, trap masters.

## Non-facts and unlimited predictions (§6)

**Non-fact.** If "rain" or "clouds" were castles in the air the hypothesis could not be tested. Every explanatory inference needs "if 甲 then 乙" with both observable in principle, however costly. The theory itself starts from abstract, non-factual assumptions; the implication must not.

The key case: the law of demand. Price is observable. Quantity demanded is an intention that does not exist in the world. The law is untestable as stated. Using transacted quantity as quantity demanded is 指鹿為馬. The correct move: derive that under specified observable conditions, 甲 leads to observable 乙, and test that. The attached conditions are test conditions, called constraints (局限條件) in economics.

**Unlimited.** If 甲 is said to produce 乙, or 丙, or 丁, or 戊 without end, nothing can refute it. Cheung: this is what disequilibrium means in economic theory, and equilibrium means the implications are constrained enough to be refutable. Price control: below-market price may bring queues, connections, violence, quality decline. Unless the theory says which, no inference exists. His 1974 price-control paper is about supplying the constraint.

Cheung's equilibrium is not physics'. A pendulum at rest is observable. Demand and supply curves are mental tools; their intersection is a concept, not a fact. Importing the physics meaning has muddled much analysis. Coase, on a 1969 drive to Seattle, wanted the term scrapped; Cheung offered the refutability reading and Coase accepted it.

Implications may be joined by "and" (stronger) or "or" (weaker), never open-endedly. Simpler chains convince more.

## Realism of theory (§7)

"Unreal" has four senses. Three are acceptable:
1. Theory is abstract. True, and necessary, since facts cannot explain facts.
2. All description simplifies. A complete description of an apple is impossible. Nitpicking this is unscientific.
3. Convenience simplifications (two-country trade model) are fine when adding a third country changes nothing material.

The fourth is not acceptable: **test conditions must be real.** The dirty test tube: a chemist may not use a dirty tube and assume it clean. If the implication holds for zero transaction costs, test where transaction costs really are negligible. Investigating and simplifying real constraints is the hardest part of economic explanation, often years of work.

## Method of economic science (§8, 4th edition additions)

- Economics is an empirical science like the natural sciences, with two differences: its laboratory is the uncontrolled real world, and economists explain themselves, so objectivity is harder.
- Hypotheses always start from a change in constraints. "甲 implies 乙" means "a change in 甲 leads to a change in 乙".
- Prediction and explanation share logic but differ in order. Explanation must pick the relevant constraint change among countless candidates, and theory guides the choice. Prediction starts from an observed constraint change that may not stay stable.
- Mathematics is not economics. A valid equation guarantees logic, not content. Statistics do not make inference more precise; precision is about agreement on measurement.
- 看不到則驗不着. Cheung accepts exactly one unobservable, quantity demanded. Modern economics multiplies unobservables (game strategies, motives, shirking, extortion, threats, concealment, opportunism) that make plausible stories with a religious flavour and no explanatory power.
- Applied-economics books start from a theory and stuff examples in. That is seeking to be right. Science seeks refutability while hoping not to be refuted. Refutable and testable are the same thing.

## The four-form grid

For "if A then C" (A antecedent, C consequent):

| Given | Infer | Valid | Role in this method |
|---|---|---|---|
| +A | +C | Yes | Prediction: constraint changed, outcome should follow |
| −C | −A | Yes | Test: outcome absent, implication refuted |
| +C | +A | No | Confirmation is not proof; survived tests corroborate only |
| −A | −C | No | Denying the antecedent; assumption realism is irrelevant to whether C follows |

Cheung weights these asymmetrically on purpose. Only −C → −A is the test. The prediction gives you something to test. The two invalid forms are the two habitual errors: treating confirmation as truth, and attacking assumptions instead of implications.
